Will These New Government Changes to Aged Care Really Help You?
March 2026

Every few years, the Australian Government announces sweeping reforms to the aged care system. There are press releases. There are ministers at podiums. There are promises about dignity, choice, and putting older Australians first.
And then families sit in hospital corridors, still confused, still being pressured to sign things they don't understand.
So when the most significant aged care reforms in a generation took effect on 1 November 2025 — replacing Home Care Packages with the new Support at Home program and introducing new residential care fees — the question worth asking is not whether the changes look good on paper. The question is: will they actually help your family?
The honest answer is: some of them will. Some of them won't. And a few of them will cost people money if they don't understand them before making decisions.
Here is a plain-English breakdown.

What Actually Changed on 1 November 2025
Three major things shifted.
First, the Home Care Package program — which had existed in its four-level form for years — was replaced by the Support at Home program. Eight classifications replaced four levels. Funding amounts changed. The way you pay your share changed.
Second, a new fee called the Non-Clinical Care Contribution was introduced for people entering residential care from 1 November 2025 onward. This is separate from the existing means-tested care fee and has its own cap.
Third, the overall framework moved toward what the government calls a "single assessment system" — one pathway to assess need rather than separate pathways for home care and residential care.

Where the Changes Genuinely Help
Interim funding while you wait
This is the most meaningful practical improvement for families. Under the old Home Care Package system, you could be assessed, approved for a Level 4 package worth $57,000 a year, and then wait six to twelve months to actually receive it. During that time you received nothing from the government unless you could access the lower-level Commonwealth Home Support Programme.
Under Support at Home, interim funding is available while you wait for your full classification to be activated. You will not necessarily receive your full budget immediately, but you are not left with nothing. For families in crisis — where mum has been assessed but is struggling at home — this is a real improvement.
More granular classifications
The move from four levels to eight classifications means care needs can be more precisely matched to funding. Previously, someone whose needs fell between Level 2 and Level 3 was either underfunded or placed in the wrong tier. Eight classifications create more steps on the ladder. Whether this translates into better outcomes in practice depends heavily on how assessors apply the new framework — but the structure is more accurate than what it replaced.
Higher funding at the top end
The highest classification under Support at Home provides around $77,700 per year. The old Level 4 Home Care Package provided around $57,000. For people with the highest care needs who want to remain at home, this is a meaningful increase — and it makes genuine 24/7-adjacent home care more financially viable than it used to be.
The End of Life Pathway
Up to $25,000 available for up to 12 weeks for people in the final period of their life who wish to die at home. This is new, it is significant, and it is something most families do not know exists. If staying home at end of life matters to your family, this pathway deserves serious attention.

Where the Changes Create New Risks
The Non-Clinical Care Contribution
This is the change most likely to catch families off guard.
If someone enters residential care from 1 November 2025 onward, they now face a new fee on top of the existing means-tested care fee. The Non-Clinical Care Contribution covers hotel-style costs — meals, cleaning, laundry, daily living. It is means-assessed, can be up to $105.30 per day, has a lifetime cap of $135,318.69, and ceases after four years regardless of whether the lifetime cap has been reached.
Here is why this matters. People entering care now face two separate means-assessed fees — the original means-tested care fee (lifetime cap $84,571.66) and the new Non-Clinical Care Contribution (lifetime cap $135,318.69). These are completely separate. Hitting one cap does not affect the other.
For high-asset individuals who previously expected to hit the means-tested care fee cap in around 2.5 years and then have fees drop dramatically — that calculation is now more complex. There is a second fee running in parallel that continues for up to four years.
This is not necessarily unfair — the government's argument is that residents should contribute to hotel-style costs just as they would if living independently. But it is a change that affects the real cost of residential care, and families making decisions without understanding it will get a financial surprise.
The eight classifications cut both ways
More precise classifications are better in theory. In practice, there is a real risk that someone previously on a Level 3 Home Care Package gets assessed as a Classification 3 or 4 under the new system — and receives less funding than they did before, because the new amounts at the lower classifications are not dramatically higher than the old ones.
If your family member has transitioned from a Home Care Package to Support at Home and their care feels like it has quietly reduced, check their new classification against their old level. They may have grounds to request a reassessment.
Complexity has increased, not decreased
One of the stated goals of the reforms was to simplify the aged care system. In some structural ways, it has. In the ways that matter to families sitting across from a discharge planner or reading a residential care agreement for the first time, it has not.
There are now more fee types to understand. There are new caps to track. There is a new contribution formula with different rates depending on whether you receive a pension, a part pension, or no pension at all. The assessment system is still administered by ACAT and ACAS with all the same regional variation in wait times and assessor judgment.
The fundamentals have not changed: the system still rewards families who understand it and quietly penalises those who don't.

What This Means for Your Decision Right Now
If your family is facing aged care decisions in 2026, here is what the new landscape means practically.
If you are considering home care, the Support at Home program is genuinely better than what it replaced — more classifications, interim funding, and higher budgets at the top end. Get assessed as soon as needs become apparent, because the assessment is the starting point for everything and earlier is always better.
If you are considering residential care, understand that the new Non-Clinical Care Contribution applies to you if entry is from 1 November 2025. Get a written estimate of both fees — the means-tested care fee and the Non-Clinical Care Contribution — from Services Australia before you sign anything. Do not assume the cost is the same as it was for someone who entered care a year ago.
If your family member is already in residential care and entered before 1 November 2025, the new Non-Clinical Care Contribution does not apply to them. Their fees are calculated under the previous arrangement. This is an important protection and it is worth confirming in writing with Services Australia.
If your family member has recently transitioned from a Home Care Package to Support at Home, check their new classification. Make sure the services they were receiving have continued. Request a reassessment if you believe their care needs have changed or their funding has reduced without explanation.

The Honest Assessment
The reforms are a genuine attempt to improve a system that has needed reform for a long time. The Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety was damning in its findings, and these changes reflect real effort to respond to those findings.
But good intentions and good outcomes are not the same thing. The families who will benefit most from these changes are the ones who understand them. The families who will be most exposed are the ones who assume that because the government has reformed the system, someone is looking out for them.
Nobody in the aged care system is specifically looking out for your family. The discharge planner has a bed to free up. The facility has a RAD to collect. The provider has a margin to manage.
Your job — and it is genuinely your job — is to understand enough to ask the right questions, slow down decisions that feel rushed, and know when the numbers don't add up.
That was true before 1 November 2025. It is still true now.
