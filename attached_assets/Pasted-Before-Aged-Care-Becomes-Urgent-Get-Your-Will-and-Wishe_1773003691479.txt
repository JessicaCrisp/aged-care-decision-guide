Before Aged Care Becomes Urgent: Get Your Will and Wishes in Place
March 2026
There is never a good time to think about this. There is always something more pressing, something more comfortable to deal with first. And then one day there is a phone call, or a fall, or a diagnosis, and suddenly the decisions that should have been made years ago need to be made in days.
This is one of the most consistent patterns in aged care — families arriving at critical decisions without the legal and personal foundations that would have made everything easier, cheaper, and less painful.
Getting your will and wishes in place is not morbid. It is one of the most practical and loving things you can do for the people who will one day have to make decisions on your behalf.
Here is what you need, why it matters, and what happens if you don't have it.
The Four Documents Every Australian Adult Needs
These four documents form the legal foundation for everything that follows. Without them, your family may find themselves unable to act — or worse, acting in conflict with each other because nobody knows what you actually wanted.
Your Will
A will determines what happens to your assets after you die. Without a valid will you die intestate, which means the government's formula — not your wishes — determines who gets what. This formula does not account for blended families, estranged relatives, specific bequests, or the informal agreements families make over decades.
A will needs to be signed, witnessed correctly, and stored somewhere your executor can actually find it. Review it every three to five years and after any major life change — marriage, divorce, the birth of grandchildren, significant changes in assets.
Enduring Power of Attorney
This is arguably more important than your will while you are still alive. An Enduring Power of Attorney appoints someone — your attorney — to make financial and legal decisions on your behalf if you lose capacity to make them yourself.
Without this document, your family cannot access your bank accounts, sell your property, or manage your finances if you become incapacitated. They would need to apply to the relevant state tribunal for guardianship and administration orders — a process that is slow, stressful, and expensive, often taking months at exactly the moment your family needs to be focused on your care.
Your attorney should be someone you trust completely. Many people appoint a spouse or adult child. You can appoint more than one person and specify whether they must act jointly or can act independently.
Enduring Guardian (or Medical Power of Attorney)
Where a Power of Attorney covers financial decisions, an Enduring Guardian — called a Medical Power of Attorney in some states — covers personal and medical decisions. This person can make decisions about where you live, what medical treatment you receive, and what care you get if you cannot make those decisions yourself.
This is the person who will be speaking to doctors, facility managers, and hospital social workers on your behalf. Choose carefully — this role requires someone who can make hard decisions under pressure and who genuinely understands what you would want.
Advance Care Directive
An Advance Care Directive — sometimes called a Living Will — records your wishes about medical treatment in advance. It can specify what treatments you do and do not want, under what circumstances you would or would not want life-sustaining treatment, and what matters most to you about your quality of life.
This document speaks for you when you cannot speak for yourself. It removes an enormous burden from family members who would otherwise have to guess — or disagree about — what you would have wanted.
Why This Matters Specifically for Aged Care
These documents are not just end-of-life planning. They are essential tools for navigating the aged care system.
When a parent enters residential aged care, someone needs to sign the Resident Agreement — the contract that commits to fees that may run to thousands of dollars per month. If the resident lacks capacity and there is no Power of Attorney in place, the facility cannot proceed, the family cannot sign on their behalf, and what should be a difficult-but-manageable situation becomes a legal tangle on top of an emotional crisis.
When a family needs to access a parent's superannuation, sell their home, or restructure assets to manage aged care costs — all of which may be necessary and financially important — none of that can happen without a valid Enduring Power of Attorney.
When doctors need to make treatment decisions for someone who can no longer communicate their wishes, an Advance Care Directive removes the anguish of guessing and the conflict of family members disagreeing.
The Conversation Nobody Wants to Have
Most families know they should have these documents. Most families don't have them — or have them but haven't told anyone where they are, or have them but they're twenty years old and no longer reflect current wishes or circumstances.
The conversation is uncomfortable. Asking a parent to think about losing capacity, or about dying, feels unkind. But consider the alternative — a family standing in a hospital corridor, one parent just admitted, the other too distressed to think clearly, and nobody with the legal authority to make the decisions that need to be made right now.
That conversation, had in a calm moment around a kitchen table, is infinitely kinder than the crisis version.
What to Do This Week
If you do not have all four documents in place, contact an estate planning solicitor or elder law solicitor this week. Not next month. This week. The cost is typically $500–$1,500 for a complete set of documents depending on complexity — a small fraction of what the absence of these documents can cost in tribunal applications, legal disputes, and financial decisions that had to wait.
If you already have these documents, check three things. First, are they current — do they still reflect your wishes and circumstances? Second, does your attorney or guardian know they hold that role and where the documents are? Third, does someone you trust know where the originals are stored?
If you are an adult child reading this for a parent, the kindest thing you can do is have this conversation now, while there is time to do it properly. Bring it up not as a conversation about death but as a conversation about making sure their wishes are respected and their affairs are in order — because that is exactly what it is.
The Bottom Line
A will, an Enduring Power of Attorney, an Enduring Guardian, and an Advance Care Directive. Four documents. One afternoon with a solicitor. The difference between a family that can act decisively when it matters and a family that can't.
Get them in place before you need them. Because when you need them, there may not be time.
